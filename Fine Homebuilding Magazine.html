Why CI?

Continuous insulation improves energy efficiency, comfort, and building durability. Learn the history and codes behind the practice.

By Randy Williams|Issue 343 - November 2026

Continuous insulation (CI) reduces thermal bridging, improves energy efficiency and comfort, and can increase building durability by helping walls and roofs stay warmer and drier. This article explains that exterior CI is generally favored for moisture control, while interior CI can also be effective when circumstances such as historic facades, cost, or climate make exterior insulation impractical. Ultimately, successful CI depends on treating the insulation as part of a complete enclosure system, with careful attention to moisture, air and water control, material properties, and continuity around openings and penetrations.

The Nuances of Continuous Insulation

Drawing on two decades as a builder, energy auditor and consultant, the author ranks added CI among the best ways to cut thermal bridging and operating costs, though savings are only part of the payoff. Strategies differ in R-value, vapor permeability, drying potential and cladding attachment.

CI entered the 2006 IRC for climate zones 4 Marine, 5 and 6, then spread with the 2012 IRC's prescriptive tables. The concept traces to products of the 1920s, and what was once a green-building niche is now standard in residential energy codes for every climate zone.

The IRC lets CI sit inside, outside or within the envelope, as long as only fasteners and service openings break it. Placement matters most: many durability and moisture benefits belong to continuous exterior insulation (CEI), though the author insulated his own northern Minnesota home from the inside.

Continuous Exterior Insulation

CEI is still the benchmark for high-performance walls because it tackles thermal bridging and moisture together, keeping framing and sheathing warmer and less prone to condensation. The price is extra thought at windows, cladding, trim and flashing.

Nailbase Panels

Panels such as Zip-R pair rigid insulation with a nailing surface, matching much of CEI's performance with easier installation, though some need separate bracing sheathing. Some question whether they are true CEI, but detailed well they perform much like it.

The Science Behind the Strategy

CI's core job is cutting thermal bridging: wood, metal and concrete move heat far faster than insulated cavities, leaving cold and warm spots that a continuous layer interrupts.

CI also improves comfort via mean radiant temperature (MRT), the average temperature of surrounding surfaces. Cold walls and windows draw body heat by radiation, so a 70°F room can feel chilly; warmer interior surfaces steady MRT.

Code-Approved Insulation Assemblies

The IECC's Table R402.1.3 offers several prescriptive wall paths. In a listing like 13+10ci, the first number is the cavity R-value and the second is the continuous layer's R-value.

The code treats interior and exterior CI alike. Some zones allow a cavity-only "30" path that may require 2×8 or 2×10 framing, and the IRC's Table R702.7(3) sets separate CEI levels tied to vapor retarders that can differ from the IECC's.

Added insulation also dampens temperature swings and equipment cycling. Durability gains come mainly from exterior CI: without it, cold-climate sheathing can stay below the dew point, gather moisture and eventually rot or grow mold.

CI in either location can also reduce outside noise, sometimes eliminate sheet vapor retarders and aid airtightness, adding up to a more comfortable, durable and efficient enclosure.

Double-Stud Walls

Double-stud walls reach high R-values with familiar framing, but studs still cross the insulation, so they aren't true CI, and cold sheathing can gather moisture if air leakage isn't controlled.

Continuous Interior Insulation

Interior CI moves the thermal break inside, mostly in retrofits where exterior work is impractical. It aids comfort but changes drying potential and complicates electrical and trim work.

Exterior vs. Interior CI

Deciding where the layer goes comes before materials or thickness. Most projects use CEI, which keeps framing and sheathing near indoor temperature and suits current moisture-control practice.

The author counts nailbase panels and Huber's Zip-R as CEI because the foam still breaks the bridge and moves the condensing surface inward; double-stud and staggered-stud walls don't qualify.

Interior CI is rarer but fits homes where facades, setbacks, roofs or budget block exterior work, and in hot-humid climates it helps prevent condensation on cool interior surfaces. The Bonfig wall (FHB #250) reduces bridging, though its insulation isn't truly continuous.

A Case for Continuous Interior Insulation

Exterior CI usually suits older homes best, but retrofits can make it costly and disruptive. The author's 1952 Cape Cod, with sloped ceilings and kneewalls built into the roof framing, had the style's usual thin insulation and air leaks, plus ice dams and high energy bills.

An over-roof retrofit would have performed best but cost more than he was ready to spend. Instead he vented below the sheathing, filled the 2×6 rafter bays with R-15 Rockwool ComfortBatt, added VaporWise as vapor retarder and air barrier, then 2 in. of ComfortBoard 80.

Done so far only at the kneewalls, the roof reaches about R-23, up from kraft-faced R-7 batts, and snow now lingers on the upgraded sections. More insulation can still be added later.

CI as Part of a System

CI doesn't replace an air or water-resistive barrier, and most failures occur at breaks around openings and transitions. Settle early where each control layer sits and how they connect.

The History of Continuous Insulation in Residential Construction

1923 — Celotex sells sheathing and other products promoted as limiting heat transfer. →

1926 — Armstrong's Corkboard offers an insulating alternative to lath under plaster. →

1935 — The Forest Products Laboratory builds some of the first structural insulated panels (SIPs). →

1942 — Architect Alden B. Dow (Dow Chemical) refines SIPs and brings them to market. →

1940s — U.S. and Canadian wartime housing trials use early phenolic and urea-formaldehyde foam boards. →

1945 — Fir-tex promotes an insulation board for interior or exterior use. →

1940s to 1950s — EPS, XPS and polyurethane foams reach the market. →

1952 — Dow Chemical patents the foam-core SIP. →

Mid-1960s — Research in both countries notes the value of insulating outside the framing. →

1970s — The energy crisis lifts interest in board insulation. →

1974 to 1979 — Canadian studies find less condensation risk with insulation outside the sheathing. →

1976 — The Illinois Lo-Cal house combines double-stud walls with triple-pane windows. →

1977 — Canada's first R-2000 program looks at exterior insulation for durability and condensation control. →

1977 — The superinsulated Saskatchewan Conservation House is finished. →

1979 — William Shurcliff dubs that era's superinsulated buildings "Micro-Load Passive," a label that later becomes "Passivhaus." →

1980s to 1990s — Canadian researchers John Straube, Joe Lstiburek, Gus Handegord, Neil Hutcheon and Mark Bomberg deepen knowledge of CI's energy and moisture effects. →

1994 — The DOE launches Building America, speeding research on thermal bridging and CI. →

1994 — Europe gets the WUFI hydrothermal model, improving moisture-risk checks on highly insulated walls. →

2001 — Oak Ridge National Laboratory works with WUFI to adapt it for North America. →

2003 — Katrin Klingenberg builds the first U.S. passive house. →

2006 — The IECC adds its first prescriptive CI requirements. →

2012 — The IECC greatly expands CI tables for wood-framed walls. →

2021 — The IECC raises insulation levels and adds compliance paths. •

Whichever side of the framing the insulation goes on, planning the whole enclosure first makes continuous insulation simpler to build and more effective.
