Duct-Leakage Testing and Why It Matters

A new home failed four duct-leakage tests, exposing unsealed connections, damaged ducts, and some lessons about when and how duct systems should be tested.

By Randy Williams | November 9, 2026

I have been conducting more duct-leakage testing recently. Our local code officials have started requiring the testing, even though the requirements have been in place in my state for more than a decade. Why the change in enforcement? I can’t say. Maybe the state provided some education, or maybe officials are seeing problems in the field caused by leaking ducts. Whatever the reason, the additional testing is helping me become better at performing the test and diagnosing problems.

A new home

Williams’s latest job involved a blower-door test plus a duct-tightness test for a roughly 1,150-sq.-ft. slab-on-grade house being built in his own city, with the ducts slated for the unconditioned attic. City officials wanted both results. The house tested at 3.56 ACH50, missing the state’s 3 ACH50 limit. The 2021 IECC added an optional CFM50-per-square-foot metric for homes under 1,500 sq. ft., but his state still follows a version of the 2012 code.

What a duct-leakage test measures

A duct-leakage test puts a number on how much air escapes from a forced-air distribution system. The tester covers every supply register and return grille, connects a calibrated fan (often called a duct blaster) to the system at the air handler or a large return, and raises the ducts to 25 pascals of pressure. The airflow needed to hold that pressure is the leakage, reported in cubic feet per minute at 25 pascals, or CFM25.

There are two common versions of the test. A total-leakage test counts every hole in the system, whether it leaks into the living space or outdoors. A leakage-to-outside test adds a blower door that brings the house to the same 25 pascals, so leaks into conditioned space stop registering and only air lost to the attic, crawlspace or outdoors is counted. Results are usually normalized by floor area and expressed as CFM25 per 100 sq. ft. of conditioned floor area, so small and large homes are judged on the same scale.

A pressure pan is a quicker diagnostic aid. With the blower door running, the tester holds a shallow pan over each register and reads the pressure behind it. A high reading points to a duct run that is well connected to the outside, which helps narrow the search before any sealing starts.

Where duct systems usually leak

Leaks tend to turn up in the same places from house to house:

  • Boots and register connections, both where the boot meets the duct and where it meets the drywall or subfloor.
  • Panned returns, where joist or stud bays are closed off with sheet metal and used as return ducts, leaving long seams and gaps against the framing.
  • Flex duct that is crushed, kinked, torn or slipped off its collar, problems that are easy to miss once insulation is in place.
  • Takeoffs and collars at the trunk lines, especially when tape is the only sealant.
  • The supply and return plenums and the air-handler cabinet itself, including the filter slot and panel seams.

Return-side leaks matter as much as supply leaks. A return leak in an attic can pull hot, humid or dusty air into the system, while supply air lost to the outside can leave the house under negative pressure, drawing unconditioned air in through the building envelope.

Sealing and retesting

Durable sealing usually pairs mechanical fastening with a sealant. Sheet-metal joints get screws, flex ducts get draw bands at each collar, and the seams are then covered with water-based mastic. Mastic can be brushed into irregular joints, and gaps wider than about 1/4 in. are typically bridged with fiberglass mesh first. Foil tapes listed to UL 181 can work on clean, dry metal, but ordinary cloth duct tape is a poor choice because its adhesive breaks down over time.

The gap between a boot and the drywall or flooring is easy to forget. It is not part of the duct, yet it lets air move between the ceiling or floor cavity and the room. Caulk, foam or a gasketed boot closes it. After any repair, the system should be tested again: fixing one leak can reveal the next-largest one, and a passing number is the only proof that the system meets its target.

When to test, and why duct location matters

Many builders test twice. A rough-in test, done after the ducts are installed but before insulation and drywall, catches problems while every joint can still be reached. A final test, after drywall, registers and equipment are in place, confirms that the finished system passes, and it is the result code officials most often ask to see.

The IECC has required duct testing for more than a decade when ducts or the air handler sit outside the building thermal envelope. Recent editions set total-leakage limits of roughly 4 CFM25 per 100 sq. ft. of conditioned floor area, with a lower target in some editions for rough-in tests run without the air handler installed, and several editions waive the test when the entire system is inside the envelope. The exact numbers depend on which edition a state has adopted and on local amendments, so it pays to confirm the requirement before the first test.

Location is why all of this matters. Ducts in a vented attic or crawlspace sit in some of the hottest and coldest air around the house, so every leak there sends conditioned air straight outdoors and can bring moisture and pollutants back in. Keeping ducts inside conditioned space is the most reliable way to limit those losses; when that is not practical, careful sealing and a passing test are the next best protection.
