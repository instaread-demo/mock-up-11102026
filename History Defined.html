Andersonville: The Prison Camp That Killed More Men Than Most Battles

By Carl Seaver

Andersonville Prison (also known as Camp Sumter) was a military prison established by the Confederates during the Civil War. Built in 1864 in Sumter County, Georgia, it remained in service for around 14 months. Within that short time, it became one of the largest (and deadliest) POW facilities in North America.

At the height of its residency, more than 32,000 inmates inhabited the prison concurrently. Of the 45,000 that eventually passed through its doors, as many as 13,000 died there (which is approximately 29% of all prisoners).

Many factors drove the death rate, and historians tend to group them under two headings: the shortages squeezing the late-war Confederacy and the camp’s weak management. The sections below trace how both took hold.

Andersonville in the Civil War

The camp grew out of the breakdown of prisoner exchanges around 1863. Earlier, captives were routinely paroled or swapped under deals such as the 1862 Dix-Hill Cartel, so neither side needed long-term prisons.

Exchanges stalled when the Union demanded equal treatment for every soldier and the Confederacy refused to recognize Black Union troops. Union advances into Georgia and Virginia also strained Southern supply lines, and with crops sent to the armies, food ran short, so authorities had more captives to feed on less. Richmond’s prisons sat in contested Virginia, so prisoners were sent deep into Georgia, far from the front, and exchanges never fully resumed.

Camp Sumter: An Administration and Infrastructure Catastrophe

The camp opened in February 1864, built for about 10,000 men, a limit its remote location soon pushed past. It was known for its “deadline,” an inner boundary roughly 19 feet from the wall that prisoners could be shot for crossing, meant to stop escapes and contact with guards and a sign of a struggling administration.

Staff were as scarce as supplies. Captain W. Sidney Winder picked the location, and a cousin, Richard B. Winder, served as quartermaster. From spring 1864 the Swiss-born Captain Henry Wirz ran the inner stockade, while the post, with its guards and stores, answered to Colonel Alexander Persons and later Brigadier General John H. Winder. Wirz’s repeated requests for food and medicine were only partly met.

At its peak the roofless pen held over 32,000 men exposed to heat, rain and cold. Some built scrap shelters called “shebangs,” but most lacked materials. One stream served for both drinking and waste, and latrines inside the enclosure could not stop contamination or disease.

Rations were mostly cornmeal, rarely meat. Dysentery, scurvy, typhoid, gangrene and dehydration were the main killers, and the small hospital outside the stockade lacked basic medicines and tools. Nearly 13,000 died, most in summer, and were buried in mass graves.

Society Inside the Prison

Survival defined each day. Guards in raised sentry boxes held morning roll calls, and prisoners were loosely grouped by state or unit.

Food arrived once or twice daily, and men cooked for themselves when fuel allowed, often eating it half done. Clothes fell apart, most went barefoot, and blankets had to choose between shelter and warmth. Cut off from home with no release in sight, morale sank.

Prisoners built their own order, including an informal elected government. “Regulators” clashed with thieving “Raiders” until, in July 1864, prisoners tried six Raiders in their own court and hanged them with Wirz’s consent. Barter then replaced theft, though walls and guards made trading risky. Union aid packages arrived by early 1865 but were handed out unevenly by a collapsing supply system.

The Aftermath of Andersonville

As Sherman neared Atlanta in September 1864, most prisoners were moved elsewhere, leaving a few thousand. The camp was evacuated in April 1865, and arriving Union troops recorded the survivors’ condition and gave food, medicine and transport, though recovery took many of them months or years.

Wirz was arrested after the war and convicted in 1865 by a military commission in Washington, D.C., on cruelty and conspiracy charges, based on prisoner testimony and surviving records.

He was executed in November 1865, the sole Confederate officer executed for how prisoners were treated. His trial is now criticized for hearsay, weak witnesses and charges against leaders never tried, and historians still weigh his blame against wider shortages. Accounts split along Union and Confederate lines; scholars note high death rates on both sides, with the Union camp at Elmira, New York losing about a quarter of its prisoners.

The grounds are now Andersonville National Historic Site, with the graves forming Andersonville National Cemetery. Union medical staff and civilians, including Clara Barton and former prisoner Dorence Atwater, used prison records to identify and mark thousands of graves, and veterans kept gathering there for decades.

The camp’s toll exceeded that of many famous battles, and no single cause explains it. Whether the fault lay with Wirz, wartime scarcity or forces beyond anyone’s control may never be settled, but the story remains a warning not to let history repeat.
