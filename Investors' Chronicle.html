Fourteen stocks with further to run

Our Double Up screen looks to capitalise on investor wariness of buying shares that have already had strong gains

by Michael Fahy

As its name suggests, the efficient market hypothesis (EMH), first laid out by the ‘father of modern finance’, Eugene Fama, argues that markets are generally very good at judging what shares should be worth.

Its basic assertion, that all of the public information that currently exists about an asset is reflected in its price, implies that in the long run there is little to be gained from studying stocks and trying to ‘beat’ the market.

If the theory held true every time, there would be little point in running this week’s screen, or most of the other screens published here. Its weak spot is the assumption that investors always behave in a fully rational way.

Three versions of an efficient market

Economists usually describe market efficiency in three strengths. The weak form says that everything contained in past prices and trading volumes is already built into today’s share price, so studying charts should not give an investor a lasting edge.

The semi-strong form goes further. It argues that all publicly available information, from company results to economic data and news announcements, is absorbed so quickly that analysing it cannot reliably produce returns above the market’s. The strong form, which few people think holds in practice, says that even information known only to insiders is reflected in prices.

Fama’s work on asset prices was recognised in 2013, when he shared the Nobel memorial prize in economic sciences. One of the economists he shared it with, Robert Shiller, is best known for research showing how investor psychology can push prices away from fundamental value. That both views were honoured in the same year says a good deal about how open the debate remains.

Why rising shares make investors nervous

A clear example of less than rational behaviour is the reluctance many investors feel about buying a share that has already gone up a long way. Part of this is anchoring: people fix on the price at which they first noticed a stock, and a much higher price later feels expensive by comparison, even when the business has improved enough to justify it.

Alongside anchoring sits a fear of buying at the top. Nobody wants to be the last buyer before a fall, and the sharper a rise has been, the louder that worry tends to become. Many investors also tend to sell winning positions early to lock in a gain, while holding on to losing ones in the hope that they recover.

Taken together, these habits can mean that good news finds its way into a share price gradually rather than all at once. If investors are slow to accept that a company’s prospects have genuinely changed, the price can keep catching up for some time after the first move.

The momentum effect

That pattern is linked to one of the most widely studied challenges to market efficiency: momentum. Research across many stock markets and long stretches of history has found that shares which have done well over the previous several months have, on average, tended to keep outperforming for a while afterwards, while weak performers have tended to keep lagging.

Academics still argue over why this happens. Behavioural explanations point to underreaction at first and herd-like buying later on, while others suggest that momentum shares carry risks investors are being paid to bear. Either way, the effect has proved persistent enough that many quantitative fund managers build it into their strategies.

How a screen for big risers can work

A screen built to take advantage of this hesitation generally starts by looking for shares whose prices have risen sharply over a set period, such as those that have roughly doubled. Price strength on its own is a blunt tool, though, so screens of this kind usually add further tests to separate rises backed by improving businesses from those driven by excitement. Typical checks include:

  • Earnings momentum: profit forecasts that have been revised upwards, suggesting analysts expect the improvement to continue.
  • Valuation: a price relative to expected earnings or growth that has not become extreme.
  • Balance-sheet strength: manageable debt and healthy cash generation, so the company is not relying on outside funding.
  • Liquidity: a minimum company size or trading volume, so shares can be bought and sold without excessive cost.

The aim is not to find the cheapest shares in the market, but companies where investors may still be underestimating how far an improvement has to run.

The risks of riding momentum

Momentum strategies have well-known weaknesses. Trends can reverse abruptly, particularly when market sentiment or interest rate expectations shift, and the shares that have risen fastest can fall hardest. When an approach becomes popular, crowding can make those reversals sharper still.

Highly rated shares also leave little room for disappointment, so a single profit warning can undo months of gains. And because momentum portfolios need regular rebalancing, dealing costs and taxes can eat into the returns of private investors who follow them closely.

Using a screen as a starting point

For these reasons, the output of any stock screen is best treated as a list of ideas for further research rather than a ready-made portfolio. Before buying, it makes sense to understand what has driven a share’s rise, read the company’s latest results and consider whether the reasons for optimism are likely to last.

Position sizing and diversification matter too, as does deciding in advance how and when holdings will be reviewed. Used in that way, a screen can help investors get past their own reluctance to back proven winners, while still applying the scrutiny that any individual investment deserves.
