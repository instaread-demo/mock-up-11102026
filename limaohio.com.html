Washington faces tough decisions on how to deliver promised economic relief

By Associated Press - November 8, 2026

By Josh Boak • Associated Press

WASHINGTON — Voters have sent a strong election-year message to the new Democratic congressional majority and President Donald Trump: Repair the damage inflation has done to the economy.

Trump, who campaigned on the idea that a Democratic victory in the midterms would trigger a 1929-style economic depression, has maintained that the economy is booming. Since his party lost in Tuesday’s voting, he has claimed he is willing to work with Democrats in the House and Senate, though that’s not something he has done much of in the past.

Trump told reporters he expects to get along with Democrats unless they turn destructive. After Friday’s report of 165,000 new jobs and 4.1% unemployment, he texted supporters that he would veto anything he sees as harmful to the country.

Democrats aim to check Trump’s power and are bracing for early spending fights, with deficits, tariffs, AI and a Social Security program facing a possible cash shortfall within six years also ahead. Marc Goldwein of the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget said change elections will likely continue until inflation and rates are under control.

Americans want lower prices

Tariffs and the war against Iran that Trump started in February helped lift annual consumer inflation to 3.7%, and the president pushed the Federal Reserve for deep rate cuts. Economist Douglas Holtz-Eakin of the American Action Forum called the Fed a surprise election winner, saying a Democratic Senate could block Trump’s Fed picks and free Chair Kevin Warsh to fight inflation. Trump says fuel costs will drop when the Iran war ends but has not said when, and Stanford’s John Cochrane said smaller deficits would aid the Fed.

The federal government keeps borrowing about $2 trillion annually

Debt keeps piling up as revenue trails spending on Medicare, Medicaid and Social Security, and yearly interest now tops $1 trillion, more than the military budget. Neither side campaigned on cuts: Trump wants $500 billion more a year for defense, and some Democrats want lapsed Affordable Care Act subsidies restored. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and budget director Russell Vought plan a post-election deficit proposal, and Goldwein said lowering Medicare costs is the easiest first step.

AI could power stronger growth, but voters have concerns about the technology

Harvard economist Jason Furman, a former top adviser to President Barack Obama, ranks AI first among economic issues; his AI tool, Econvitals, found data center construction up 80.6% in a year as other building fell. Stocks slid on fears Democrats could restrict data centers, while Trump favors light regulation even though most respondents in The AP Voter Poll said AI hurts the economy more than it helps. Furman warned that leaning on one source of strength also creates a weak point.

Social Security needs to be rescued

Social Security is projected to fall short of full benefits at the end of 2032, and options such as lifting the payroll tax cap or raising the retirement age would likely need bipartisan votes. Sen. Elizabeth Warren, D-Mass., is drafting a cap plan with Ohio Republican Sen. Bernie Moreno and cited experts who say checks could shrink by nearly a quarter after 2032.
